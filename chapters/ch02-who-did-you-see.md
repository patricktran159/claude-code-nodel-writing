## 2. Who did you see?

![Two masked people stand two metres apart on either side of a street planter of striped tulips at golden hour, a magpie on the lamp post above](images/c02.png)

"Question two," Elly reads. "Who did you see?"

"One person. That was the rule."

"Was it Dad?"

"Your father used to cook for me every night."

"Dad still cooks. He made me an omelette shaped like Tasmania."

"Yes," I say. "He does."

On the shelf behind her a photograph lies face down. Elly hasn't mentioned it. I haven't turned it over.

"Write: *one person*," I say, and she does.

&nbsp;

It took five weeks. In the telling it takes five minutes, which is the first lie in any story.

They kept time by the press conference. At five to eleven every morning Theo came up the stairs with a bowl under a tea towel, and at eleven they watched the Chief Minister on Nell's laptop with the volume low, like a couple listening to a relative's will. Numbers. Exposure sites. A new phrase each week that everyone would be sick of by Friday. At 11:02, through the wall, Beryl knocked twice. Nell knocked back once.

They walked the one hour the law allowed, in masks, two metres apart, around the loop of Kingston that passed the lake and the shuttered shops. It was spring, and the magpies had decided, in the way of Canberra magpies in September, that Theo's head was private property. He took to wearing a colander. She took to walking on the far side.

On the fifth Thursday, Beryl opened the door of 4.11 as Theo passed with a bowl, looked him up and down like a customs officer, and said, "About time." Then she shut it.

Theo stood on the landing holding the soup. "Was that approval?" he whispered.

"With Beryl," said Nell, "it's the only form there is."

The first dinner was a Tuesday. He cooked it in her kitchen, because hers had the longer bench, from whatever she had: eggs, a lemon on its last day, rice, a stock cube she had been saving for an emergency.

"My mother makes this when there's nothing," he said, whisking. "Lemon and egg and whatever's left of the rice. She calls it *nothing soup*. It's the best thing she makes."

"That sounds like a policy position."

"It's a philosophy." He held the spoon out to her. She tasted it. It tasted like being looked after, and it arrived so suddenly that she put the spoon down.

They ate at her table with the window open and the lake going dark. He asked the question she had known was coming, and had prepared for, in the spreadsheet.

"Why me?"

"Flour," said Nell. "I explained in the memo."

"It was a good memo. Why *me*, though?"

"You appeared low-risk."

"I appeared —" He started to laugh. "That's twice. You said it in the note." He looked at her over the bowl, and she saw that he had decided, kindly, not to press. "Okay. Low-risk. I'll take it."

She did not mention Immy. She did not mention cell B14, or a dishwasher, or the pencilled table in her planner on which he had been a circled word. It was the first omission she counted. It was not the last, and not the biggest, and by then he was ladling, and she let it go cold in her mouth like something she had forgotten to swallow.

She ate without wiping the table. She noticed afterwards. She let the crumbs stay.

He found her labels unbearable, and then, by the third week, essential. *SALT. SALT (COARSE). SALT (DO NOT).* He asked her to label his flour. He carried a glass jar between their kitchens that he called Barry, a sourdough starter that needed feeding every morning, and Theo, who had never kept anything alive for longer than a lease, fed it at six with the devotion of a monk. Barry commuted. Barry had the best social life in the building.

He taught her to make the bread on a Sunday, with both hands in the dough, standing behind her at the bench.

"How much water?"

"Enough."

"Is there a ratio?"

"There's a feeling."

"Is there a number for the feeling?"

"Nell." His hands covered hers. They were warm, and rough, and entirely certain, which was the thing she could not model. "You're not going to get it wrong. The worst that happens is it's flat. Flat bread's still bread."

She would have liked that to be a rule. She tried it on, like a coat. It fitted in some places.

He left a card on her bench afterwards. A recipe, in his quick slanting hand.

*Don't stir it. Trust the heat.*

She kept the card. She kept most things.

Her mother rang each evening at six with the Sydney numbers, which that month had the quality of weather in a war zone. "And you're keeping to yourself?" Mo said. "Obviously," said Nell, which was true in the sense that she was keeping to one person, and which she said in the voice she used for a statement that was accurate but not complete. She heard herself do it. She had never lied to her mother. She had only ever let things be understood.

The first time he touched her was an accident: his hand on her hand, on the lid of a jar. The second time was not.

In the fourth week, they sat on the landing of the stairwell, because the lift took one person at a time and the stairs had a better echo. It was past ten. He had his back against the concrete and she had hers against the rail.

"My father went to Perth when I was fourteen," Theo said. "He sent postcards. Mum put them on the fridge for a year, and then she took them down and made spanakopita and didn't talk about it for a decade." He turned his hands over, studying the flour in the creases. "So I got good at leaving first. Saves everyone the postage."

She told him about the hardware store. The car park. A heart nobody had checked. She had never told it in so few words.

"That's the worst story I've ever heard," he said.

"Technically, it's not the worst."

"Nell."

"It's very bad," she conceded.

He laughed. Then he sang the first line of "Fly Me to the Moon" up the shaft of the stairwell, and it came back down off the concrete like a thing that had been waiting.

On the way down, she passed the door of 2.07, which stood a hand's width open, and through the gap she saw that the four boxes in his hallway had not moved. KITCHEN. KNIVES. BOOKS. MISC. Still taped. Still labelled in the quick slanting hand.

In four weeks he had never once asked her down. They ate in her kitchen and walked her streets, and he took himself home to the room she had never seen, like a man going back to a ship.

She did not ask. Asking was an unquantified risk.

&nbsp;

On Tuesday, 14 September, at eleven, the Chief Minister said *a month.*

They were on her sofa, with a cushion between them, as there had been for four weeks. It had never been discussed. Barr said the fifteenth of October. The laptop breathed. Theo laughed.

It was the laugh he used when he was frightened. She had learned it the way you learn the sound a fridge makes before it fails.

"A month," he said. "Well. I'm not going anywhere."

He said it lightly. She didn't know she had heard it until much later.

She shut the laptop. Her watch said 11:24. Outside, a car alarm started and stopped, and in the flat below, someone began a vacuum cleaner, which seemed to her the bravest sound she had heard all year.

"Technically," Nell said, "I think I'm in love with you. I'd like it noted."

She heard her own voice do it. It was the voice she used for the likelihood of harm. She'd said the most frightening sentence of her life as if reading a finding, and she could not make it stop.

Theo laughed. Then he stopped laughing.

The fridge hummed. Through the wall, Beryl's television explained something to someone. Nell looked at her watch, because that was what her eyes did. Eleven seconds. Eleven seconds in which a man who had never stayed anywhere looked at the floor, then the window, then her hands.

"Noted," he said.

Then he took the cushion from between them and put it on the floor.

I have kept those eleven seconds. Other people keep photographs.

They did not do anything else that afternoon. That was the strange part. They sat with their knees touching, like two people who have just survived something, and watched the light cross the lounge room. Later, the other thing happened, carefully, like people reading instructions that turned out to be unnecessary. Afterwards, in the dark, he asked if he could sing.

"No," said Nell.

He sang anyway, very quietly, into her shoulder.

&nbsp;

She told Immy that night, on the phone, with the speaker on and her feet up.

"How's the dishwasher?" said Nell.

"Magnificent. Marcus has opinions about grout. So it's the flour man."

"His name is Theo."

"I know his name. I've heard it nineteen times this week. I'm counting." Immy laughed her loud laugh, and then it stopped, which was the noise Immy made when she was about to be a good friend. "Nell. Can I say something?"

"Technically —"

"Don't use your work voice. I'm saying this because I love you." A breath. "Nobody falls in love in a lockdown. They just run out of other places to be."

Nell looked at the card on the bench. *Trust the heat.*

"Thank you for your assessment," she said.

"That's the voice."

"I know."

She hung up. She did not ring Immy again for three days, and Immy, who knew her, did not ring either.

&nbsp;

Floriade was cancelled that year. The bulbs were already in the ground, so they came up anyway, three hundred thousand of them, in parks nobody could gather in and in planters left outside the shut shopfronts of the suburbs.

On the last Sunday of September they found one on the corner of Giles Street. Red, orange, striped like deckchairs. No one around. A magpie sat on the lamppost and thought about it.

They stood the legal distance apart, in masks, and looked.

"They didn't get the memo," Theo said.

Above the mask his eyes were creased, delighted, entirely unguarded. She would have crossed the two metres. The law said no. So she stood at the distance the law allowed, and loved him across it, which turns out to be a perfectly workable arrangement for about a month.

That night Nell wrote the date in her planner. *26 September.* She didn't write why. I don't need to. I could find that corner of the pavement with my eyes shut.

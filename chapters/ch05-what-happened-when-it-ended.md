## 5. What happened when it ended?

"Question five," Elly reads. "What happened when it ended?"

"They opened the restaurants."

"Did you go?"

"Eventually."

"That's not a story," Elly says, with contempt. "That's a timetable."

"Technically —"

"*Mum.*"

Friday, 15 October. The last night.

They sat on Nell's balcony, in the cold, in the two chairs that had never been sat in at once. Below them, Kingston held its breath. The lights of the other flats lay about in squares: a man ironing, a couple arguing without sound, a child standing at a window in a dinosaur costume for no reason that anyone would ever know. Down on the lake road a single car went by, slowly, with its windows down, as if it didn't trust the silence either.

She had her planner on her knee. She had not opened it. He had Barry on the floor between his feet, in his jar, with the lid loose.

At 11:59 pm the rule would let go.

She had expected a feeling. She had written the day into the spreadsheet weeks ago, in a cell she had coloured gold, and now it was here and the cell was only a cell. At midnight nothing happened. A clock changed. Somewhere in the city a law stopped being a law. Someone on the second floor banged a saucepan with a spoon, three times, and then thought better of it.

Earlier that week, on the corner of Giles Street, the tulips had started to go. Petals lay on the pavement like dropped tickets. Nobody had come for them. They had bloomed anyway, and now they were finishing anyway, and she'd found that a comfort and a threat in the same breath.

In the last days they had played a game. It was the sort you play when you don't want to play the other one.

"First thing," Theo had said. "I'm going to stand in Dom's kitchen and smell the extractor fan."

"I'm going to get my hair cut."

"That's not a first thing. That's a medical necessity."

They each said a first thing. Neither of them said a second.

"That's it, then," Theo said.

"Technically," said Nell, "there are still a number of restrictions —"

"Nell."

"I know."

The city did not come out. It stood in its windows. It had been shut in for nine weeks and did not yet know how to leave.

That afternoon, taking the recycling down to the basement, she had seen that the four boxes were no longer in his hall. She had found them in the storage cage between a bicycle and someone's artificial Christmas tree. Taped. Labelled. KITCHEN. KNIVES. BOOKS. MISC. Not thrown out and not unpacked. Put where things go when you haven't decided.

She knew a number, and she was not going to say it. He knew it too. The Hobart offer had seventeen days left on it. It was the only number in the building that nobody had said aloud, and she could feel it between them like a third chair.

Her phone buzzed on the arm of her chair.

It was a text. From him. From a man sitting two feet away, looking at the lake.

*Staying. Is that okay?*

She read it once for the facts. Once for the tone.

The facts were that he had texted it, a man who sang on stairs and could talk to anyone about anything, because it was the only way he had found to say it that he could stand to have been said. The tone was a question mark. It was the most frightened punctuation in the language.

She looked at him. He looked at the lake.

There were things she could have said. *I love you* — she had said it already, once, in September, to a man who had said *noted.* *Is it me, or is it the rule?* *If it had never happened, if there had never been a lockdown and a note and a closed border, would you have stayed?* *Would I have knocked?*

She said, "It's cold."

He took off his jacket and put it on her shoulders and she let him. It smelled of flour and soap, of Barry and flat 2.07, of a man who had been inside for fourteen days and had never once complained.

She leaned, an inch and then more, until her head lay on his shoulder. He went still, the way a man does when a bird has landed on him.

"Don't sing," said Nell.

He sang. Four bars, so low they were more breath than tune, into her hair.

She opened the planner. It fell open where it always did. On the left, her entries for the week. On the right, the little table she had ruled in August with a steel ruler:

*Immy* — struck through.
*Colleague, level 2, Barton* — 0.4.
*Marangos* — *Unknown.*

She uncapped her pen. She held it over the word. She knew exactly what the new word was. That was the trouble. She had spent her whole career learning not to write down what she knew until she could prove it.

She wrote the time instead.

*12:04.*

I have thought since about that word. I have several candidates. None of them is a number.

At 12:10 her phone lit up again. *IMMY: WELL??* She turned it face down on her knee. It was the second thing that night she did not answer.

At 12:20 he fed Barry. Flour, water, three fingers of salt from the pinch he'd never lost. He took up the spoon.

"Don't stir it," said Nell. "Trust the heat."

"I know what I wrote," said Theo, and stirred it.

He did not ask what she had written. He sat still, with his hands round the jar, and she understood that he had decided not to look, and that this was a kind of courage, and that it was also, possibly, a kind of exit.

"They opened the restaurants," Elly writes. She looks at it. "That's all you said."

"It's what happened."

"Mum."

"We stayed," I say. "That's the answer."

It's a true sentence. It's also a sentence that fits on a worksheet, and I have written enough briefs to know the difference between a finding and the truth.

"What happened after?" Elly says.

"Then you were born."

"That's *it*?"

"That's the part that fits."

She lets it go, as she lets most things go, which is a trait she did not get from me. She flattens the worksheet. She turns to the last box, which has a smaller font and a clip-art tree.

"*Is there anything you want to tell people in the future?*" She reads it with the voice of a person who has been waiting for this. "Okay. Go."

"Be careful," I say.

She writes it down. Her pencil stops.

"Dad said to put *tell people early*," she says. "I think he meant about homework."

"Did he."

"Do you want to change yours?"

"No," I say. "Be careful. It's what you tell people."

She looks at it. She doesn't cross it out. She doesn't add anything either, and I feel the room do what rooms do when a child has noticed something and has decided, with great tact, to say nothing.

On the shelf behind her, the photograph is still face down. I know what is in it. A woman at a kitchen table, reading, who has no idea she is being looked at. She is not smiling. She is, if you look closely, almost happy. He took it on a Monday in September, at lunch, and told me it was for the light. I laid it face down one evening, and I have not been able to stand it up again.

Tomorrow is Sunday. Tomorrow, at ten, he will come to the door and feed a jar on the bench that is a year older than she is. He won't say much. On his way out, because he can't help it, he'll hum four bars of something into the hallway, and I will stand at the bench with my back to the door and not turn around until it stops.

"Mum?"

"Mm."

She hasn't looked up. The pencil is a long time still.

"Is that why you and Dad aren't together?" Elly says. "Because you were only scared then?"

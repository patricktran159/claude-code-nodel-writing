## 4. What did you miss most?

"Question four," Elly reads. "What did you miss most?"

"Honestly? Hairdressers."

"Mum. That's not a feeling."

"It's the most truthful one I've got."

She writes *HAIRDRESSERS*, with the face of a person making notes on a witness.

On the bench, the jar has risen to the lid and is pushing at it. "Barry's hungry," Elly says.

"Leave him. Someone will be along on Sunday."

"Dad says Barry's older than me."

"He is," I say. "By about a year."

Quarantine is mostly furniture.

For fourteen days Flat 2.07 became the whole of the world, and the world had one chair. Nell worked from the bench with her laptop on a chopping board. Theo cooked with the window open, in the cold, because someone had told him ventilation mattered and he believed it with the devotion of a man who had never been told anything useful before. They took turns with the chair. They took turns being right.

The press conference came through a phone propped against a flour tin. Beryl knocked on the wall of a flat they were no longer in. A man in a visor swabbed Theo at the door on the first morning and left without touching anything, and in the afternoon the news came that it was negative, which made no difference. It was fourteen days regardless. It was one of the things that had never been explained to her satisfaction: that a negative was not an answer, only a pause.

She labelled his pantry on the third day. FLOUR (BREAD). FLOUR (OTHER). He read the labels aloud as if they were poems. He asked her to do the spices. She did the spices. She had not been so happy in a year, and she knew it, and it frightened her.

On the sixth night neither of them could sleep. They lay on the bed in the dark, in their clothes, because it was cold, and a sleeping bag lay over their knees like a chaperone. He made tea on the hot plate and poured it into two mugs, and they toasted, solemnly, with tea.

"*Yia mas*," Theo said. "It means *to our health*."

"A lot to ask, this month."

"A lot to ask every month."

She had been looking, all week, at the postcard on the fridge. A river. A bridge. The colours of a place that was always having a good day. In the dark, on impulse, she got up and turned it over.

*Theo — weather good here. Dad.*

That was all. Four words and a signature, in the hand of a man who had, apparently, said everything he'd meant to.

"It's the only one I kept," he said from the bed. "Mum took the rest down. I don't know why I kept that one. Maybe because the weather's always good."

She turned it back, carefully, so the river faced out. She did not say what she was thinking, which was that a man who keeps one postcard for twenty years is not a man who has forgotten how to stay. He is a man who has been practising for a very long time.

In the second week his mother rang from Queanbeyan on speaker, a voice like a kitchen. A cousin in Kingston had left a tray at the street door and fled. Spanakopita, still warm in foil. "You sound thin," Roula said. "Are you eating? Is she feeding you?"

"She labels things," said Theo.

"Good. Somebody should." A pause on the line. "Your father also said he'd stay," Roula said, as though she were remarking on the weather, and Theo took the phone out to the balcony.

"What does that mean?" Nell said when he came back.

"It's a thing she says."

She wrote it down. She did not know what column it went in.

&nbsp;

On the ninth day, at noon, his phone buzzed on the bench.

He was in the shower. The water drummed. Nell was holding a container of lemons she had just labelled, and she did what she would have said she never did: she looked. Not at the phone. At the glass. At the light that came up through the screen and stayed there, as lights do, a little longer than you want them to.

It was an email. The preview ran four lines.

> **Re: Sous Chef, Harbour Street Kitchen, Hobart**
>
> Hi Theo, hope lockdown's treating you okay. Just following up. We've kept the position open through the travel restrictions and can hold it until 1 November. Could you confirm your start date by then? We'd love to have you. — Ines

She read it once for the facts. Then she read it for the tone, and the tone was *we'd love to have you*, and that was worse.

Hobart.

She looked at the doorway of the hall. Four boxes. KITCHEN. KNIVES. BOOKS. MISC. She had stepped around them for nine days. She had put them in the column marked *Unknown.* She had been so careful not to ask.

The water stopped.

When he came out, in a towel, hair standing up, singing a bar of something, she was standing exactly where she had been, holding the lemons.

She put the container on the shelf. Label out.

"When were you going to tell me?"

He saw the phone. He didn't need to look at the screen.

"I wasn't," he said.

It was the second honest thing he'd said that fortnight, and she noticed that it was a very good one.

"That's the truth," Theo said. "I was going to be gone. And you were going to have a very clean plan."

"On the fifteenth of August."

"A Sunday. I had the ticket. Four boxes, a lease I'd already given notice on. And then the lockdown came, and then there was a border, and then —"

"And then there was me."

"And then there was a note under my door that had *principal* in it."

Her mouth moved. She didn't let it.

"You said you weren't going anywhere."

"I wasn't. I'm not."

"Is that a promise," Nell said, "or a weather report?"

He opened his mouth. He closed it.

"I don't know how to tell the difference," he said. "I've never had to."

"Would you have gone?"

"On the fifteenth? Yes. That's the true answer."

"And now?"

He looked at his hands. There was a little flour in the creases, always, even in the shower; she had never worked out how.

"I don't know," Theo said. It was the first completely honest thing he'd said since September, and she did not know whether to be glad.

On the eleventh night she propped her phone against the flour tin and played a song, very low, because of the walls, and held out her hand.

"You don't dance," said Theo.

"I'm assessing the risk."

There was room for seven steps one way and three across. She had counted. He counted her in under his breath, and she said, "You can't count a dance," and he said, "Everything's countable. That's your whole theory," and she said, "Then count." He did. By the fourth bar she had stopped counting, and by the fifth she had stopped noticing that she'd stopped, and they turned in the small room between the bed and the hot plate, in their socks, under the eyes of the labelled containers, until the song ended and neither let go.

She had not forgiven him. She danced anyway. It was possible, she found, to do both. She wished someone had told her sooner.

&nbsp;

On the twelfth day they swabbed him again at the door. On the thirteenth, the text came.

*Your result: Not detected.*

They were sitting on the bed. It was the only place to sit that wasn't the chair. Nell read the message, and he read it, and neither of them moved. She had thought there would be a noise. She had thought, in the quiet logic of her planner, that relief was a thing that arrived by itself, like a bus.

"Well," Theo said.

"Well."

"We should do something."

"Yes."

She checked her watch. It said 4:12. She got up and straightened the containers on the bench, and moved SOUP in front of BREAD, and when she turned around he was looking at her with a face she'd seen before, on a man in a stairwell, singing to nobody.

"It's not funny," Nell said, "that I'm tidying."

"I didn't say it was."

"You were going to."

"I was going to say," he said, "that it's the nicest thing anyone's ever done in my kitchen."

She laughed. It came out as a sob and she turned it into a cough. He crossed the room in two steps and she let him, and they stood in the flat the size of a lift and held on to each other, and the negative sat between them like a thing neither of them had earned.

At midnight on Monday his fourteen days were over.

Beryl, who held her spare key and had no sense of boundaries, had sent clothes down in carrier bags all fortnight, without comment, like a smuggler. In the morning Nell went up herself, for the first time in fourteen days. Her flat looked like a diagram of someone else's life. On the balcony, the herbs were dead. She had watered them by schedule for three years, and in a fortnight they had gone brown at the edges and folded over, like hands. She stood and looked at them for what her watch said was a long time.

Four more days of the rule that let her see him, and no one else. In four days the rule would let them go anywhere at all.

Neither of them said what that meant.

## 3. Were you scared?

"Question three," Elly reads, and stops. She has underlined it twice. "Were you scared?"

She takes the pencil out of her mouth.

"You're allowed to say yes," she says. "Dad did. He said he was scared the whole time and that's why he cooked."

"Did he."

"Mum."

I turn the pancake over. It comes apart, as the ones I make do.

"I'm going to do this one properly," I say. "Let me tell you about a Monday."

* * *

&nbsp;

What I know of that morning I know from time stamps.

At 6:02 on Monday, 27 September, his phone made a sound it had never made before. A text, from a number that wasn't a person. *ACT Health. You have been identified as a close contact of a confirmed case of COVID-19. You must quarantine for 14 days.* Then a link, and a list of places. One of them was Dom's takeaway counter in Braddon, on Saturday night, between 6:40 and 7:20, where Theo had handed a bag of arancini across a table, at arm's length, in a mask, to a man who had been standing in a queue with a cough he thought was hay fever.

At 6:09 he recorded a voice note. It is seven seconds long. He did not send it.

At nine o'clock he came up the stairs with a bowl under a tea towel, as he did every morning, and Nell thought nothing of it.

She thought nothing of it for ten hours.

He was funny. That was the first thing she noticed, much later, going back over it as she went back over all her failures, looking for the line in the margin. He was a little too funny. He fixed the dripping tap in the bathroom, which had been dripping for a year, and then he fixed the one in the kitchen, which had not. He washed his hands twice before he touched the bread. He stood behind her while she worked at the table, and put his chin on the top of her head, and stayed there a long time, until she said, "Theo. I'm in a *meeting*," into a laptop where nine colleagues pretended not to hear.

At lunch he took her photograph while she was reading. She heard the little electronic click and looked up. "What was that for?"

"Nothing," Theo said. "The light."

She let it go. She let a great many things go that day, because every one of them, taken alone, was nothing.

At two, between calls, he leaned on the doorframe of the lounge room with a tea towel over one shoulder.

"What's the likelihood?" he said.

"Of what?"

"Of harm. Say it the way you do in the brief."

She had the voice for it. She had a whole drawer of that voice. "The likelihood of harm," said Nell, "is assessed as low."

He closed his eyes. "That's the nicest thing anyone's ever said to me."

She laughed. He watched her do it, and went on watching a beat too long, with an expression she filed under *tenderness* and would later, with some bitterness, move to a column of its own.

At four her mother rang. Nell took it on the balcony, in her work voice. Mo read out the numbers. "And you're well?" she said.

"Perfectly."

"And you're not going out?"

"Not once," said Nell, and heard herself say it, and looked through the glass at a man in her kitchen making a salad he did not need to make.

At seven the phone lit up on the bench.

It was his, face up, next to the olive oil. Theo had gone out to the balcony to feed Barry the evening cup of flour, which he did not usually do on the balcony. The screen showed a message from Dom.

*Tell her tonight mate. You're good right up until it gets real. Get real.*

Under it, still on the lock screen, was the one from 6:02.

She read it the way she read everything. Once for the facts. Once for the tone.

The facts were that it was nineteen minutes past seven. The facts were that she had been in a small flat with a close contact for ten hours. The tone, when she checked, was her own.

&nbsp;

He came in with the jar. He saw her face and set the jar on the bench the way you set down something that might go off.

"What time did you get that?" she said.

"Six-oh-two."

"It's seven."

"I know."

"That's thirteen hours."

"Nell —"

"You've been in my kitchen all day." Her voice was calm. It was the calm that frightened her. It was the one she used for the worst paragraph in a brief. "You touched my face."

"I know."

"You put your chin on my head."

"I know. I was going to tell you after breakfast. Then after the meeting. Then after you'd eaten. Then it was lunch and you laughed at the thing with the tap and I thought —"

"You thought?"

"That it was the last time you would."

The fridge hummed. In the flat below, the vacuum cleaner that had been the bravest sound of the year started up again, and she hated it.

"I recorded something," he said. He took out his phone, thumb unsteady. "At six-oh-nine. I never sent it."

He pressed play and held it out between them like an exhibit.

*"Hey. It's me."* A pause. The sound of a hot plate ticking. *"Never mind. I'll tell you later."*

Seven seconds.

"That's it?" she said.

"That's the whole confession."

She nearly laughed. It rose in her like something coming up through a drain, and she shut her mouth on it, because if she laughed she would have to do the other thing too.

"Go downstairs," Nell said.

He went at once. He did not argue. At the door he said, "You're not even a contact. Technically. I looked it up. You're a contact of a contact, so you don't have to —"

"Go downstairs, Theo."

&nbsp;

She sat in her flat for an hour. She had built it for exactly this: a controlled environment, a clear plan, everything labelled. She sat in the silence she had made, and she tried to calculate.

The probability that he had been infectious on Saturday night: low. He had been masked; the window was forty minutes. The probability that she had been infected, if he had been: lower. The probability that going down the stairs would change either number: nil.

She had been taught that a decision made on the numbers is a decision made well.

She had also been taught, at twelve, in a hardware-store car park, what the numbers were worth.

*Leave a tray at the door,* she thought. It was what her mother would have done. It was what any sensible person would have done. And it was, she saw with surprise, exactly what she was about to do.

She made a plan. It took four minutes. Soup, bread, apples, tea, the good tea bags. She took the containers from the shelf and labelled them in black marker. SOUP. BREAD. APPLES. TEA. She stacked them in a tray in order of weight, because there was a right way to do this, and the right way was to leave a tray at a door and walk away.

She took the stairs. They had an echo, and she heard every step arrive.

On the second floor she put the tray down on the mat of flat 2.07. She stepped back two metres, to the line the building had taped on the concrete. She could hear him on the other side. Not singing. Not doing anything. A man in a studio flat with four taped boxes, being quiet at her.

She stood there for sixty-one seconds. I know, because she looked.

She had written a hundred briefs on the difference between actual risk and perceived risk. She had never once found herself on the wrong side of the arrow.

I tell Elly that Nell *decided to stay*. It's a better word than the one I've got. The truth is that Nell did not decide. She went down the last two metres the way you reach for something that is falling.

She knocked twice, quietly.

He opened the door in the dark, hair flat on one side, in a T-shirt he'd slept in. "You can't be here."

"I know."

"I'm — Nell, the whole point is —"

"I don't forgive you."

"Okay."

"I'm not saying it so you'll say okay."

"Okay," he said, and then, as she picked up the tray and walked past him into a flat she'd never entered, "I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I didn't want you to send me away."

"I'm not sending you anywhere," Nell said. "That's the problem."

She put the containers on his bench, in a row, with the labels out.

"It's a policy," she said, "not a protection. Upstairs isn't safer than here. It's just further away."

It was the first time she had been inside. One window. One chair. A hot plate, a mattress made tight as an envelope, a shelf of knives with their handles all facing the same way. On the fridge, one postcard, curled at the edges, of a river and a bridge. *PERTH.* She didn't turn it over.

They put their masks on, absurdly, in a flat the size of a lift. For the first hour they sat at either end of the room like two people at a bad wedding, and neither said anything, and a magpie on the sill took it in turns to look at each of them.

"Is there anything else?" Nell said at last. "Now. Before I take this off."

"No," said Theo.

He said it in under a second. I have timed it since.

She took her mask off.

&nbsp;

She woke at three. She had taken the bed because he had put her in it, and he had taken the floor, because the rules did not stretch that far, and because he was a man who, in some corner, believed that a floor was the right place for a person like him.

He wasn't on the floor. He was sitting on the end of the bed with the knife roll across his knees. Not cutting anything. Holding it, the way you hold something someone has asked you to mind while they go away.

"You came down," he said.

"I know."

"Why?"

She had an answer ready. She had several. She was a person who always did.

She gave him none of them. She took the knife roll off his knees and set it on the floor, and moved over on the narrow bed, and lifted the corner of the sleeping bag.

It was not an answer. It was a different kind of document.

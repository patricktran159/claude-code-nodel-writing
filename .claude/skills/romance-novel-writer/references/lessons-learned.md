# Lessons Learned from a Full Run

These come from building and reviewing a complete short romance with this workflow (an 8,000-word, five-chapter story). They are the mistakes and fixes most worth knowing before you start. Each is a transferable habit, not a rule about that story.

## 1. Brainstorming

- **Show a working card after every round** and let the user react to it. A concrete draft beats an abstract question.
- **Recommend a default** in each question; make it the first option.
- **Give the user real choices, then lock them.** Keep a "decisions locked" table so earlier choices aren't re-litigated.
- **When the user says "use your assumptions", decide, and record each assumption in a table** (question, assumption, reason) so it can be revisited.
- **Research real settings before drafting.** Dates, rules and local texture lift the story, and mistakes are embarrassing. Cite sources; tag the unverified.
- **Do not invent specifics about real institutions.** Invent venues, building names and alert wordings, and flag them.

## 2. Premise and structure

- **Container Formula:** a universal love situation, plus a constraint that blocks the obvious solution, plus a clock. The best premises generate conflict without a villain.
- **Give the telling a motive.** A frame (a child's homework, a letter, an interview) makes the story necessary and gives the teller something to hide.
- **Make the ambiguity doubled.** Two competing questions (was it love or fear? did he stay or was he kept?) let an open ending feel earned rather than evasive.
- **Write the private truth as a causal timeline.** Hidden backstory should be logical (cause → effect), blameless on both sides if the tragedy is meant to hurt, and only *seeded*, never narrated.
- **Pick a hinge scene** (the crisis where a secret surfaces and a choice costs something) and give it the most room.
- **Compress time with summary; spend scenes on turns.** In a short form, one chapter can hold weeks as a montage plus two scenes.

## 3. Characters

- **Each lead's flaw must cost something on the page.** If one lead's flaw is only a quirk, a later reversal that depends on it will feel unearned. Dramatise it with a short scene beat (about 150 words).
- **Give every lead a mirror** (an animal, a plant, an object) that changes with them.
- **Differentiate voices by syntax and habit** (a signature phrase, evasion through jokes, formal qualifiers), not heavy dialect.
- **Keep supporting characters to one function each**, and cut them to one line in short forms.
- **A closed interior can be a strength.** Letting one lead speak only through documents (texts, notes, a recipe card) keeps their motives open.

## 4. Drafting to a word budget

- **Write to a per-chapter budget and measure** (`scripts/measure_prose.js`). A first draft often lands at 75–85% of target.
- **Pad with scenes, never with filler.** Good additions: a concealment on the other lead's side, an object that pays off later, a quiet intimate beat, a sensory moment at a window.
- **Draft the last line of each chapter first.**
- **Place documents deliberately** (a memo, an email, a voice note, a text), at most one or two per chapter.

## 5. Style traps the measurements caught

- **Prose drifts short and clipped.** In the test story, sentences averaged 8.5–10 words and about half were five words or fewer, against a 12-word and 25% baseline. Merge short sentences into rolling ones at emotional peaks; keep fragments for hooks.
- **Dialogue creeps up.** Nearly half the paragraphs opened with speech. Add physical business and sensory place to scenes.
- **Signature phrases wear out.** A phrase repeated eight times needs each use to change meaning, or it becomes a tic. A short list of recurring images that transform is a motif; a repeated flourish is a mannerism.
- **Narrator asides need rationing.** A few "I have timed it since" moments are powerful; a dozen dilute the effect.
- **Explaining the emotion the image already carried** is the style's recurring weakness. Cut the explanatory clause.

## 6. Making romance and tragedy memorable

- **Romance** lives in specific, repeatable gestures: a memo, a double knock, a cushion that is moved to the floor, "Don't sing." (and the song), a counted dance. Plan three to five of these per story.
- **Restraint before release.** Spend pages denying the thing the reader wants, then give it a small, exact form.
- **Tragedy needs a seen loss.** Show happiness before taking it, or include one concrete glimpse of what was lost. Telling the reader a couple later separated is not enough.
- **Plant a small object early; pay it with changed meaning at the end.** A photograph taken "for the light", then laid face down years later, did more than any statement could.
- **End on an image or a question, not an explanation.** A child's unanswered question, or a ritual the narrator cannot turn around to watch, lingers.
- **Decide whether the ending is a surprise or a dread.** If the reader will guess early, lean into dramatic irony on purpose.

## 7. Continuity errors to hunt for

- **Geography and floors.** (A character on the top floor cannot hear "the flat above.")
- **Weekdays and durations.** Check dates against a calendar; check "X days" arithmetic.
- **Cultural fit of details.** (A restaurant with an Italian name should not serve Greek skewers unless intended.)
- **Supplies and logistics.** (If a character is stuck in another's home for two weeks, where do their clothes come from?)
- **Tense and voice slips** in frame-and-story structures.
- **Counts that echo by accident.** (Two different "eleven"s in nearby scenes.)

## 8. Review and revision

- **Review against evidence**: numbers from the script, quotes from the text.
- **Fix objective errors straight away; propose subjective changes.**
- **Rank weaknesses by reader impact.** Expect the same few categories: rhythm, an under-dramatised flaw, an implied rather than felt loss, a thin setting in the middle chapters, an over-signposted reveal, tic saturation.
- **Keep revisions small and specific**, and say what is cut to pay for what is added.
